NS14

Created By SailHub

Overview

The NS14 is an Australian restricted development class of sailing dinghy, designed in 1960 and known for its lively performance, family-friendly handling, and strong development culture. It is a fast two-person boat that can be tuned and refined within class rules.

Boat figures

Length overall4.27 m
Beam1.6–1.8 m
Hull weight64 kg
DraftApprox. 1.09 m with centreboard down
Sail area9.3 m² upwind
Jib/genoa area2.3 m²
Mainsail area6.9–7.0 m²
Crew2
NotesRestricted development class; no trapeze or spinnaker

Brief history

The class was developed in Sydney, Australia, and introduced at the Northbridge Sailing Club before spreading to other states and eventually New Zealand and the United States. Its design evolved over time through a strong development tradition, including early wing masts and increasingly refined hull shapes.

Key features

  • Designed around a two-person crew and built to be competitive without a trapeze or spinnaker.
  • Offers a good balance of performance and accessibility for novice and family crews.
  • Allows significant development within class rules, including innovative rig and hull improvements.
  • Known for efficient rigs, self-draining cockpits, and a strong emphasis on fast, modern sailing.

MG14

Created By SailHub

Overview

The MG14 is a two-person, high-performance skiff-style dinghy developed in Australia. It combines a restricted-development hull with a single trapeze and asymmetric spinnaker, making it fast, tactical, and rewarding for skilled crews.

Boat figures

Length overall4.25–4.30 m
Beam1.60–1.83 m
Hull weight64 kg
DraftApprox. 1.09 m with centreboard down
Sail area9.3 m² working sail area
SpinnakerAsymmetric spinnaker, approx. 14 m perimeter
Crew2
NotesSingle trapeze; high-performance development class

Brief history

Originally developed in 1963 at Manly Sailing Club in Sydney as the Manly Graduate 14ft, the MG14 was created as a stepping-stone between smaller junior boats and larger skiffs. It evolved from a double-luff spinnaker setup to the modern asymmetric spinnaker design and remains a popular class in several Australian states.

Key features

  • Designed as a high-performance two-person boat with a single trapeze and lively downwind speed.
  • Uses a restricted-development hull and sail rule set, helping older boats remain competitive while still allowing refinement.
  • Offers a strong mix of technique, strategy, and pace rather than relying purely on brute force.
  • Related closely to the NS14, but with the added performance of trapeze and spinnaker sailing.

Laser

Created By SailHub

Overview

The Laser is a single-handed, one-design dinghy designed in 1970 by Ian Bruce and Bruce Kirby, with Hans Fogh creating the sail. It is known for its simple rig, durable fibreglass construction, and outstanding racing performance.

Boat figures

Length overall4.23 m
Beam1.37 m
Hull weight59 kg
DraftApprox. 0.79 m
Sail area7.06 m² (Standard)
Rig optionsStandard, Radial, 4.7
Crew1
NotesOne-design class with interchangeable rigs

Brief history

The Laser emerged from a desire for an affordable, easy-to-transport racing dinghy. After early development and testing, it was introduced in the early 1970s and quickly became one of the world’s most widely sailed one-design classes. It has since become a staple of club racing, coaching, and Olympic sailing.

Key features

  • Built as a lightweight, car-top-friendly boat that is easy to launch, store, and transport.
  • Uses a common hull with interchangeable rigs: Standard (7.06 m²), Radial (5.76 m²), and 4.7 (4.7 m²).
  • Designed for one sailor, with simple handling and strong performance in a wide range of conditions.
  • Widely raced worldwide and has been an Olympic class since 1996, with Laser Radial used in women’s Olympic sailing.

Rolling Tack

Created By SailHub

Overview

The Rolling Tack is a sailing technique used to change the boat's direction by rolling the boat on its side, allowing it to sail closer to the wind.

How to Perform

  1. Move weight to leeward side
  2. Push tiller same direction
  3. Move weight to windward side
  4. The boat tacks
  5. Stay stilling on the old windward side until your butt goes in water
  6. Ball move in unison to windward, flattening boat
  7. Sheet in

Brief history

The Rolling Tack technique has been used in sailing for many years, allowing sailors to navigate more effectively in various wind conditions.

Rolling Gybe

Created By SailHub

Overview

The Rolling Gybe is a sailing technique used to change the boat's direction while sailing downwind, allowing for a smoother and more efficient gybe manoeuvre.

How to Perform

  1. Roll boat windward and bare away slightly
  2. Pull and flick the main across as you change sides
  3. Flatten the boat
  4. Correct the steering

Brief history

The Rolling Gybe technique has been developed and refined over the years by sailors seeking to improve their downwind performance and maintain control during gybes.

Backwards Sailing

Created By SailHub

Overview

The Backwards Sailing technique is used to navigate a boat in reverse, typically in situations where traditional sailing methods are not feasible.

How to Perform

Entering
  1. Sail into wind, until boat slows
  2. Push boom windward
  3. Steering is reversed
Exiting
  1. Push tiller towards boom
  2. Pull tiller to stop spinning
  3. Pull main sheet and sail forwards

Brief history

The Backwards Sailing technique has been used by sailors for many years, particularly in tight spaces or when manoeuvring in harbours where forward sailing is not possible.

Vang, Cunningham, Outhaul

Created By SailHub

Overview

The Vang, Cunningham, and Outhaul systems are essential for controlling sail shape and performance in various wind conditions.

Vang

The vang pulls boom down, controls leech tension (back edge) and shape at middle of sail. The general rule is more vang is more curve, as wind increases.

Cunningham

The cunningham (Downhaul) pulls the tack of sail down, moves depth of sail forwards, and flattens the top of the sail.

Outhaul

The Outhaul pulls the clew of the sail out, flattens the bottom of the sail, and reduces the power of the sail.

Setting in Different Wind Conditions

Wind strength Cunningham Vang Outhaul Shape
Very light Light Light Medium curved
Light Light Very light Light Very curved
Medium Medium Light Medium Flat
Strong Hard Hard Hard Very flat

Brief history

The Vang, Cunningham, and Outhaul systems have been developed over time to provide sailors with precise control over sail shape, allowing for optimal performance in a variety of sailing conditions.

Navigational Marks

Created By SailHub

Overview

Navigational marks are essential tools for safe maritime navigation, helping sailors identify hazards, channel boundaries, and important features on the water.

Lateral Marks

Lateral marks identify the edges of a channel and show which side of the channel should be followed.

  • Port marks are red, can-shaped buoys with red lights.
  • Starboard marks are green, cone-shaped buoys with green lights.
  • When travelling upstream, keep green marks on your starboard side and red marks on your port side. When returning from sea, the direction is reversed.

Cardinal Marks

Cardinal marks identify the safe side of a hazard. Their black and yellow patterns indicate whether the safe water lies to the north, east, south, or west of the mark.

The lights flash in patterns based on the position of a clock face: continuously for north, three flashes for east, six flashes for south, and nine flashes for west. The south cardinal mark uses six flashes followed by a long flash.

Special Marks

Special marks identify specific areas or features, such as underwater pipes, moorings, swimming areas, or points of interest. They are yellow, have a yellow X-shaped topmark, and display a yellow light in any rhythm. They may also be used as lateral marks when their shape indicates a channel boundary.

Isolated Danger Marks

Isolated danger marks identify a specific hazard surrounded by generally safe water, such as a wreck, rock, or underwater intake. They have two black spherical topmarks and are black with a red band in the middle.

Brief history

Navigational marks have been used for centuries to aid sailors in safely navigating waterways. Their designs and colour schemes have evolved over time to provide clear and standardised guidance for mariners worldwide.

Pacer

Created By SailHub

Overview

The Pacer is a 3.8 m one-design, two-person sailing dinghy originally designed by Jack Holt. It is widely used in Australia for training, school and university sailing, family sailing, and competitive racing. Its relatively light hull, wide beam, moderate sail area, and optional spinnaker give it a useful balance of stability and performance.

Mast height5.6 m
Mainsail area6.0 m²
Jib area1.9 m²
Spinnaker area7.4 m²
Crew2
NotesOne-design sloop; commonly used for training and racing

Brief history

The Pacer was designed by British dinghy designer Jack Holt and was originally commissioned as a family sailing dinghy. The design became established internationally and has developed a particularly strong presence in Australia, where Pacer fleets are used by sailing clubs, schools, universities, and racing associations.

Key features

  • Designed for two sailors with a relatively stable and forgiving hull.
  • Light enough for two people to move and transport.
  • Uses a mainsail and jib with an optional asymmetric-style spinnaker.
  • Suitable for beginners while still providing useful performance for racing.
  • Popular as a training and team-racing boat in Australian sailing programmes.

Brief history

The Corsair was designed by Australian yacht designer Alan Payne and was originally built in Sydney from the late 1960s. Fibreglass construction and a comparatively heavy hull gave the boat considerable strength and stability. The design became particularly useful for sail training, including Australian naval cadet programmes, while also supporting class racing.

Key features

  • Well suited to training because of its predictable and forgiving handling.
  • Can accommodate additional sailors during training or recreational use.

Australian Sharpie

Created By SailHub

Overview

The Australian Sharpie is a fast three-person planing dinghy that evolved from the 12-square-metre Sharpie class used at the 1956 Melbourne Olympic Games. The modern Australian Lightweight Sharpie became established during the 1960s and has retained its distinctive hull shape while developing a powerful modern rig. It is renowned for close racing, high speeds, and demanding crew work.

Boat figures

Length overallApprox. 6.1 m (20 ft)
BeamApprox. 1.42 m
Minimum hull weight90 kg
Working sail planMainsail and jib
SpinnakerYes
Crew3
TrapezeUp to one trapeze on each side of the mast
NotesLightweight Australian development of the 12 m² Sharpie; planing hull

Brief history

The Australian Sharpie evolved from the 12-square-metre Sharpie that competed at the 1956 Melbourne Olympics. Australian sailors and designers subsequently developed a lighter version, which became a national one-design class during the 1960s. The class has maintained its characteristic hull form while allowing refinements to construction, sails, spars, and equipment within class restrictions.

Key features

  • Large three-person dinghy capable of high-speed planing.
  • Powerful mainsail, jib, and spinnaker combination.
  • Designed around coordinated three-person crew work and hiking/trapezing.
  • Hull shape has remained substantially unchanged since the development of the Lightweight Sharpie in the 1960s.
  • Strong Australian class association with fleets and championships across multiple states.
  • Known for competitive racing across a wide range of wind conditions.
